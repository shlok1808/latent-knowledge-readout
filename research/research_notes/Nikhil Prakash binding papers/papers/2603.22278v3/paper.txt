A BSTRACT
Many multimodal tasks, such as image captioning and visual question answering,
require vision–language models (VLMs) to bind objects with their properties
and spatial relations. Yet it remains unclear where and how such associations
are computed within VLMs. In this work, we show that VLMs rely on two
concurrent mechanisms to represent spatial variable binding. In the language model
backbone, intermediate layers represent content-independent spatial relations on
top of visual tokens corresponding to objects. However, this mechanism plays
only a secondary role in shaping model predictions. Instead, the dominant source
of spatial information originates in the vision encoder, whose representations
encode the layout of objects and are directly exploited by the language model
backbone. Notably, this spatial signal is distributed globally across visual tokens,
extending beyond object regions into surrounding background areas. We validate
the generalization of our findings to complex natural images from the COCO
dataset, where globally amplifying the vision-derived spatial representations across
all image tokens corrects spatial variable binding failures across models of various
sizes. Together, our results clarify how spatial variable binding is computed within
VLMs and highlight the central role of vision encoders in enabling it.

I NTRODUCTION

Spatial variable binding, the ability to associate objects with their properties and their relations to
other elements, is a fundamental component of multimodal reasoning (Cheng et al., 2024; Chen et al.,
2021; Zheng et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2024; Goetting et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2025). Tasks such as
image captioning, visual question answering, and visual navigation require vision–language models
(VLMs) to bind objects to spatial relations and reason about their relative arrangement within the
scene. Although recent advances have made VLMs highly capable across many tasks, spatial variable
binding remains a significant challenge (Campbell et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025; Wen et al., 2024;
Kamath et al., 2023). In this work, we investigate the internal mechanisms underlying spatial variable
binding in VLMs and show that the mechanisms identified in controlled settings also account for
model behavior in complex natural scenes.
In language models (LMs), recent studies have shown that associating entities with their attributes
(often referred to as variable binding) is done by forming symbolic representations for each entity in
context. This representation encodes content-independent information about the order of entities in
context, and allows the model to distinguish between entities and retrieve entity-specific information
when needed (Dai et al., 2024; Prakash et al., 2024; 2025). Follow-up studies suggest that VLMs
similarly rely on ordering information when reasoning about objects in images (Assouel et al., 2025;
Kang et al., 2026). However, while these findings establish the use of ordering-based representations
during multimodal reasoning, they do not reveal their origin. In particular, it remains unclear whether
such representations are constructed within the LM backbone, inherited from the vision encoder, or
emerge from interactions between the two. Identifying the source of these representations is essential
for understanding spatial variable binding failures in VLMs and potentially developing principled
methods to diagnose and correct them.

We show that the ordering-based representations underlying spatial variable binding in VLMs arise
from two concurrent sources: The vision encoder represents the global layout of objects in the
image, encoding ordering information that is directly projected into the embedding space of the LM
backbone. Then, the LM backbone can further augment these representations by forming ordering
information over object-associated visual tokens. While the vision encoder provides the primary
source of ordering information, the LM-side mechanism plays a secondary role, augmenting spatial
ordering when that information in the vision embeddings is degraded or completely removed. Notably,
the ordering information provided by the vision encoder is distributed globally across visual tokens,
extending beyond object regions into surrounding background areas. This is in contrast to the LM
backbone, where information is formed locally over object-related tokens, similar to the process
found in language processing (Prakash et al., 2025; Assouel et al., 2025).
We establish these results through a series of controlled interchange intervention experiments (Vig
et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022; Geiger et al., 2022) using carefully constructed counterfactual
samples across three synthetic datasets and one controlled naturalistic dataset (Kamath et al., 2023).
We validate our findings across five transformer-based VLMs from three model families (Qwen,
Gemma, and Pixtral), showing that they rely on similar mechanisms to solve spatial variable binding.
Finally, we demonstrate that this mechanism extends to natural images: a simple global intervention
that amplifies vision-derived ordering information across all image tokens corrects up to 55% of
previously incorrect predictions on complex, in-the-wild COCO scenes with diverse objects and
spatial relations (Lin et al., 2014; Kamath et al., 2023) (see Fig. 12).
Overall, our results elucidate the mechanisms underlying spatial association in VLMs, connect
binding mechanisms identified in LMs to multimodal reasoning, and demonstrate the central role of
vision encoders in supporting spatial variable binding in VLMs. All code and data supporting this
study are available at https://spatial.baulab.info.

R ELATED W ORKS

Benchmarking Spatial Reasoning Many benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate spatial reasoning in VLMs, including synthetic datasets designed to test compositional relations and naturalistic
benchmarks derived from real images and human annotations (Kamath et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024;
Gholami et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025). These benchmarks have revealed that spatial reasoning remains
challenging for current VLMs, particularly as relational complexity increases or when reasoning must
generalize beyond object-centric cues.
Understanding Inner Working of VLMs A growing body of work has investigated the internal
workings of VLMs, examining cross-modal attention patterns, token alignment, representational
geometry, and information flow between vision encoders and LM backbones (Nikankin et al., 2025;
Qin et al., 2025; Assouel et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025; Neo et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2024; Kaduri
et al., 2025; Rott Shaham et al., 2024; Cohen et al., 2026). These studies have provided valuable
insights into how visual and textual information is integrated and how multimodal representations
emerge. Our work builds on this line of research by focusing specifically on the mechanisms
underlying spatial association and reasoning.

Symbolic Representation in LMs & VLMs Several studies have shown that LMs implement
variable binding by forming content-independent ordering identifiers, over important tokens (GurArieh et al., 2025; Prakash et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2024; Prakash et al., 2024; Feng & Steinhardt,
2024; Davies et al., 2023). These mechanisms underlying variable binding are fundamental to
many in-context reasoning tasks and have been presented as evidence of symbol-like processing
in neural networks. Recent work has extended this line of inquiry to VLMs, investigating similar
symbolic representations (Kang et al., 2026; Assouel et al., 2025; Saravanan et al., 2025; Hasani
et al., 2025). Our findings relate to this body of work while revealing an important distinction in
the multimodal setting. We show that although VLMs form symbolic representations that encode
ordering information within the LM backbone, they only act as a supporting mechanism. Instead,
symbolic representations originating from the vision encoder play a dominant role.

E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP

3.1

DATA

We study spatial variable binding across increasing visual complexity, from synthetic and controlled
settings to complex natural images (Fig. 1). The controlled settings are necessary to enable causal
analysis, which requires constructing original-counterfactual input pairs that differ in exactly one
factor. The naturalistic settings then provide evidence that the findings apply to real-world conditions.
Synthetic Settings We consider three synthetic settings: Squares, Shapes, and Objects. In all
settings, three equal-sized items are placed at fixed, equal distances and arranged either horizontally
or vertically. The settings differ only in visual content: Squares uses colored squares, Shapes uses
colored geometric shapes, and Objects uses real-world object images. All images are generated
programmatically from predefined collections of colors, shapes, and objects (see full list at App. A),
enabling precise control over attributes and spatial configuration. For each image, we query the VLM
to predict an attribute (color, shape, or object name) of a target entity (square, shape, or object) based
on its relative position to a reference entity. For our intervention experiments (see Sec. 5), we sample
100 clean–counterfactual image pairs from each setting and report 95% bootstrap confidence intervals;
we validate the statistical reliability of this sample size in App. C.4. Importantly, we further validate
that our results generalize to images with real-world backgrounds (App. C.11) and to diagonal spatial
arrangements (App. C.5).
Naturalistic Setting For mechanistic analysis, we use the control subset of What’sUp (Kamath et al.,
2023) which consists of images containing pairs of objects: a central reference object (e.g., a chair)
and a secondary object positioned adjacent to it on either side. To adapt this dataset to our intervention
experiments (Sec. 5), which require images containing three objects, we construct composite scenes
by merging pairs of What’sUp images into a single image with a shared reference object and two
surrounding objects, resulting in 1074 images. This construction supports only horizontal spatial
relations. In Sec. 5.4, we evaluate generalization to natural images from COCO-spatial (Kamath
et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2014), curated by filtering the COCO validation set for images that exhibit
spatial relations. This results in 2687 images with complex scenes containing multiple objects, varied
spatial arrangements, and realistic backgrounds. We also report generalization of these results to the
on/under and front/behind relations using the original What’sUp images.
3.2

M ODELS

The mechanistic study is performed with five transformer-based VLMs from three model families: Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct, Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct,
Gemma-3-4b-it, and Pixtral-12B. These models differ in scale, training data, and visionencoder design (in particular, the positional encoding of the vision encoder; see App. C.6.2), allowing
us to assess whether the identified mechanisms generalize across diverse VLM designs. The behavioral performance of the models on the spatial variable binding tasks is summarized in App. A.1
(Table 2). In the main text we present results for Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct; results for all other
models are reported in App. C.1 and App. C.3. For causal experiments, we use examples where the
model answers correctly, ensuring the identified mechanisms reflect genuine behavior rather than
noise. Nevertheless, we show generalization of the findings to failure cases in Sec. 5.4.

3.3

M ETHODS

Representation Probing A probe (Belinkov, 2022) is typically a lightweight classifier trained
to predict a target property y from an internal representation h ∈ Rd extracted from the model. In its
simplest form, a linear probe takes the form ŷ = Wh + b, where W and b are learned parameters.
Successful probing that generalizes to test cases indicates that the target information is linearly
decodable from h, suggesting that it is present in the model’s representation.
Causal Mediation Analysis Interchange intervention is a technique for testing causal relationships
between a model’s internal representations and its behavior (Vig et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022).
Let f (·) denote the model, and let hℓ (x) denote the internal representation at component or layer
ℓ produced during a forward pass on input x. Given an original input x and a corresponding
counterfactual input x′ , an interchange intervention replaces hℓ (x) with hℓ (x′ ) while keeping the
remainder of the computation
 unchanged (i.e., activation patching). This yields an intervened output
ŷint = f x; hℓ ← hℓ (x′ ) . If the intervened output ŷint matches the target outcome associated with
the counterfactual input, this provides evidence that the intervened representation hℓ plays a causal
role in the model’s computation. We quantify this effect using interchange intervention accuracy
(IIA) (Geiger et al., 2022). Rather than measuring binary agreement with the intervention target as in
the original IIA, we average probability assigned to the target intervention outcome token.

P RELIMINARIES

4.1

O RDERING I NFORMATION IN LM S

Variable binding is the process of associating attributes with their respective entities. For instance,
given a red, green, and blue square, the process of assigning the color feature to each square involves
variable binding. Recent works have investigated the underlying mechanisms that enable variable
binding in LMs (Prakash et al., 2025; Dai et al., 2024). A common finding is the reliance of
LMs on content-independent ordering representations to bind entities to their attributes. These
representations encode the order of entities independently of their content, and align this ordering
with a corresponding order of attributes in the context, enabling the model to retrieve entity-specific
information when queried. For instance, given a prompt such as “Box A contains an apple, box B
contains a banana, and box C contains cherries. What is the color of the fruit in box B?”, the model
uses ordering information corresponding to the second entity (Box B) to retrieve the attributes of the
corresponding second fruit (banana). Prior analyses show that such ordering information is formed
in intermediate layers on top of entity-related token positions and is subsequently transferred to the
final token position, to retrieve the relevant property (e.g., the color “yellow” of the banana).
4.2

O RDERING I NFORMATION IN VLM S

In VLMs, spatial variable binding is a fundamental capability central to many spatial reasoning
tasks. Recently, Assouel et al. (2025) demonstrates that when VLMs are queried about objects within
images, they retrieve symbolic representations that encode their ordering information. While this
work establishes the existence of such ordering representations for visual variable binding tasks, it
does not characterize the source of it. Specifically, the work mainly shows that for variable binding,
the model uses this symbolic representation at the last token position, and that it comes from visual
tokens; however, it remains unclear where and how VLMs create ordering information. In particular, it
remains unclear whether ordering representations are constructed within the LM backbone, inherited
directly from the vision encoder, or emerge through interactions between the two components. In this
work, we show that the source of this is two-fold: the vision encoder encodes positional information
to capture spatial layout, while the LM backbone further enhances this information.

5.3

LM BACKBONE E NHANCES O RDERING I NFORMATION

In the previous sections, we showed that visual token embeddings produced by the vision encoder
and projector module already encode object ordering information. We now ask what role, if any, the
7

Preprint

LM backbone plays in spatial variable binding. Namely, does the LM backbone merely consume
ordering information from the vision components, or can it generate such information when needed?
5.3.1

R EMOVING O RDERING I NFORMATION FROM V ISION E MBEDDINGS

To isolate the contribution of the LM backbone, we first ablate ordering information from the
visual token embeddings using the interventions illustrated in Fig. 8. Specifically, we apply two
modifications: (1) we replace the embedding of each square with an embedding of the same-colored
square taken from an image in which that square appears alone in the middle of the image, thereby
preserving color information while removing relative ordering; and (2) we replace all backgroundtoken embeddings with those from an empty image, eliminating ordering information encoded in
background regions. Together, these interventions effectively remove ordering information originating
from the vision encoder, while preserving object colors. We do not apply this intervention to the
What’sUp dataset, as constructing a precise intervention is challenging for natural images.
As shown in Table 6 (App. C.2), removing ordering information from the vision embeddings leads
to a substantial drop in performance across datasets, confirming the central role of vision-derived
ordering information. However, performance remains above chance (33.3%), suggesting that the LM
backbone can compensate for the missing ordering signals.

We conduct additional experiments to check the generalization of these results to further naturalimage settings. (i) Transferability: position probes trained on object images, as in Sec. 5.2.1, recover
the strip-like ordering patterns on What’sUp images (App. C.10) and on synthetic objects over real
backgrounds (App. C.11). This indicates that the ordering information captured by probe directions
is transferable across object and image settings. (ii) Correcting more failures: amplification also
corrects 43–55% of failures on the What’sUp composites (App. C.7.2) and 53–68% for additional
object relations such as “on/under” and “in front/behind” on the original, unstitched What’sUp images
(examples in Fig. 43; App. C.7.3). This indicates that the findings also hold for more complex object
relations, including depth. The gains persist when α is selected on a validation split and evaluated
on held-out failures (App. A.4). (iii) Content independence: a probe trained on What’sUp corrects
COCO-spatial failures as well as an in-domain probe (48% vs. 47%), and a horizontal probe corrects
vertical failures nearly as well as a vertical one (33% vs. 35%; App. C.7.4), indicating a shared
ordering code rather than dataset- or axis-specific features. Together, these results indicate that the
mechanism identified in the controlled synthetic settings also governs spatial variable binding in
complex natural scenes.

C ONCLUSIONS

We show that spatial variable binding in VLMs relies on ordering representations arising from two
complementary sources: a primary signal encoded by the vision encoder and a secondary mechanism

formed within the LM backbone. By isolating and intervening on these representations, we show that
the same mechanism governs natural images, where amplifying them corrects binding failures. Our
results highlight that mechanistic understanding can improve model performance.

A.4

NATURAL -I MAGE VALIDATION – E XPERIMENTAL SETUP

Following the dataset construction described in Sec. 3.1, we evaluate the ordering-amplification
intervention from Sec. 5.4 on the What’sUp composites and COCO-spatial examples.
For What’sUp, each composite image contains 3 objects, yielding two two-option forced-choice
questions; querying the object to the left and to the right of the middle reference object. For
example, a possible query for the leftmost image in Fig 11 is Which object is to the left of the chair:
a book or a lamp? Answer with one word. and the other query is Which object is to the right of the
chair: a book or a lamp? Answer with one word.

Figure 11: Examples for images from What’sUp control (Kamath et al., 2023)
For COCO-spatial, each pair of objects serves as subject–reference for querying, and is converted
into a binary spatial-relation question: left/right for horizontal relations and above/below for
vertical relations. This formulation reduces dependence on open-ended object naming or recognition.
For example, a possible query for the leftmost image in Fig 12 is Is the dog to the left or to the right
of the newspaper? Answer with one word. and the other query is Is the newspaper to the left or to the
right of the dog? Answer with one word.

Figure 12: Examples for images from COCO-spatial (Kamath et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2014)
To control for answer-option order bias, each query is evaluated twice, once with the answer options
A and B in the query, and once with the reverse order: option B and A (for example Is <subject>
to the left or to the right of <reference> and then again with reverse answer options: Is <subject>
to the right or to the left of <reference>) An example is counted as correct only if both queries are
answered correctly; otherwise, it is treated as a failure. Under random guessing, this strict criterion
yields a 25% chance-level accuracy.
Amplification directions are obtained from the position probes described in Sec. 5.2.1. For
What’sUp, baseline-correct data consists of composite images for which both directional examples, left and right, are correct under the strict two-ordering protocol. We train a three-class
left/middle/right position probe using visual-token embeddings from the left side-object,
central reference-object, and right side-object strips. For COCO-spatial, we train separate two-class
probes for horizontal and vertical relations using baseline-correct subject–reference examples. The
original COCO bounding boxes of the subject and reference objects are mapped to visual tokens,
whose embeddings are mean-pooled within each box to obtain object-level representations for probe
16

Preprint

training. Probes are trained as linear classifiers with AdamW (lr=10−3 , weight_decay=10−4 ) for 200
epochs.
As described in Sec. 5.4, the intervention amplifies all the learned ordering directions in the visual
embeddings. For each failure example, we test only the queries that were incorrect before amplification (e.g., asking if the subject is to the left or to the right of the reference, and not about whether it is
to the right or to the left if the second was answered correctly). The example is counted as corrected
only if all previously failed query(s) become correct under the intervention. If the model originally
failed under both queries, the same amplification coefficient α must correct both.
We use consistent notation across all amplification tables in Sec. C.7. P ROBE and R ANDOM denote
the fixed-α setting, where a single coefficient is selected across all examples. P ROBE * and R ANDOM *
denote the per-example setting, where each example may use the coefficient that yields its best
correction outcome, serving as an upper bound on intervention performance. We report postintervention accuracy and corrected-failure rate, i.e., the fraction of baseline failures corrected by the
intervention.

B

A DDITIONAL D ISCUSSIONS

B.1

L IMITATIONS .

This work focuses on a small set of spatial relations as a minimal setting that enables rigorous causal
analysis. The mechanistic analysis covers left/right and above/below, and extends to diagonal arrangements in App. C.5; the amplification experiment additionally covers the on/under and front/behind
relations of What’sUp (App. C.7.3). More complex configurations, such as “between” or hierarchical
relations, and a mechanistic account of depth relations such as front/behind, are left for future work.
The mechanistic analysis is further limited to controlled and composite settings, as causal interchange
interventions require paired inputs that differ in exactly one factor — a condition that cannot currently
be satisfied with unmodified natural images. Nevertheless, the amplification experiment of Sec. 5.4
extends to complex natural images from COCO-spatial. Several of the additional analyses are run on
a single model: the probe-transfer experiments (App. C.7.4) on Qwen2-VL-2B and the diagonal
analysis on Qwen2-VL-7B. Finally, while we validate our findings across five open-weight VLMs,
results on closed-source models or architectures with fundamentally different vision encoders remain
unexplored. We view these as important directions for future work.
B.2

B ROADER I MPACT S TATEMENT

This work underscores the critical role of the vision encoder in multimodal systems, challenging
the prevailing focus on the language backbone. By demonstrating that the dominant symbolic
representations for spatial variable binding originate in the visual components, we highlight that
future advances in VLMs must prioritize the training of robust vision encoders to ensure accurate
spatial layout encoding. Furthermore, we show that the identified mechanisms account for model
behavior on natural images, where amplifying vision-derived spatial signals corrects spatial binding
failures without additional training or fine-tuning.
Methodologically, our discovery that spatial information is diffused across multiple visual tokens,
extending even into background regions, signals a necessary paradigm shift for interpretability
research. Standard analysis techniques that focus on single tokens are insufficient for capturing these
distributed representations. As the field moves toward reasoning models that generate increasingly
long sequences of tokens, developing interpretability methods capable of analyzing information
distributed across multiple tokens will be essential for diagnosing and enhancing model behaviors.

V ISION T OKEN PATCHING

In this subsection, we present the results of the interchange intervention experiment described
in Section 5.2.2. We report results for interventions that patch both the square and strip visual
tokens for each model. The findings are shown in Fig. 19, Fig. 21, Fig. 18, and Fig. 20. In both
cases, the results suggest that ordering information is not sufficiently encoded in the square visual
token embeddings. Figs. 24–29 report the same two interventions for Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct,
Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, and Pixtral-12B. Strip patching flips the prediction in all three
models while square-only patching does not, but the layer at which the flip appears differs across
models, and comparing it with the vision-ablated experiment of Sec. 5.3.2 distinguishes two cases.
In Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, patching the projected visual embeddings alone has no effect, and
the flip appears from LM layer 1 onward (Fig. 27). This does not indicate that the LM backbone
constructs the ordering information itself: when the vision-derived ordering information is ablated
(Fig. 30, bottom rows), the LM backbone’s own ordering representation flips the prediction only
in layers 12–23, well after the layer-1 onset of the strip-patching flip. The ordering information
therefore still originates in the vision encoder; it enters the LM backbone through its first layers rather
than through the input embeddings alone, consistent with Qwen3-VL’s DeepStack design, which
injects intermediate vision-encoder features into the early LM layers in addition to the projected
embeddings.
In Pixtral-12B, by contrast, neither the projected embeddings nor the first seven LM layers
carry a transferable ordering signal: strip patching flips the prediction only in LM layers 8–17
(Fig. 29; cleanly for the horizontal arrangements and for Shapes, weakly for the remaining vertical
arrangements), and this is the same window in which square-token patching flips the prediction after
the vision-side ablation (Fig. 31). In Pixtral the LM backbone thus appears to construct the ordering
information itself, with little contribution from the vision encoder. This may also explain Pixtral’s
low accuracy on the synthetic tasks (Table 2): without a vision-derived ordering signal the model has
to rely on the weaker LM-side mechanism, and ablating the vision-side ordering information does
not consistently reduce its accuracy (Table 6).
All curves are means over n=100 pairs with 95% bootstrap confidence bands (Pixtral: original n=50
runs).
In Fig. 23 and Fig. 22, we present additional patching results after removing vision encodergenerated ordering information from visual embeddings using the procedure from Section 5.3.1.
Output switching occurs in intermediate layers, corroborating our finding that the LM backbone
produces its own ordering information. Figs. 30 and 31 show the same experiment for the remaining
models. Under the ablation, the per-direction n (panel titles) is capped by the accuracy of the ablated
model, so several cells have n ≪ 100 and correspondingly wide confidence bands; cells with no
valid pairs are left empty.

S AMPLE S IZE AND S TATISTICAL S IGNIFICANCE

All interchange-intervention curves in this paper use n=100 clean–counterfactual pairs per direction
and setting, with 95% percentile-bootstrap confidence bands (B=2000 resamples over pairs). An
earlier version of these experiments used n=50 pairs. Because pairs are kept only when the model
answers both the clean and the counterfactual prompt correctly, the achievable n is bounded by model
accuracy; the ordering-ablation experiment of Sec. 5.3.2 and several Pixtral cells therefore have
n < 100 (reported in panel titles).
To check that the sample size does not change the conclusions, we compared the n=50 and n=100
estimates cell by cell. For each (experiment, model, setting, direction) we take the peak-layer effect,
P (counterfactual answer) − P (clean answer) at the layer where it is maximal, and test whether it
differs between the two sample sizes with a two-sided permutation test (104 shuffles). Where the
n=100 pairs are a superset of the n=50 pairs, we test the original 50 against the 50 added pairs;
otherwise the two samples are treated as independent draws. Table 7 summarizes the 132 direction
cells (119 testable; 13 ablation cells admitted no new pairs). The median absolute change in the peak
effect is 0.010; 5 of 119 tests reach p < 0.05, matching the ≈ 6 expected by chance, none survives
Benjamini–Hochberg correction, and 129 of 132 cells have overlapping 95% confidence intervals.
The n=100 results thus reproduce the n=50 results and merely tighten the intervals.

D IAGONAL A RRANGEMENTS

The main text studies axis-aligned relations (left/right, above/below). To test whether the same
mechanism handles relations that are not aligned with the token grid, we construct two additional
Squares settings in which the three squares lie on the principal (top-left to bottom-right) or the
secondary (top-right to bottom-left) diagonal of the image, and query them with compound relations
(“above and to the left of”, “below and to the right of”, “above and to the right of”, “below and to
the left of”). Strips are defined analogously to the axis-aligned case as diagonal bands (the set of
tokens with a fixed value of row+column, or row−column, that pass through a square). We run all
four causal experiments on Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct with n=100 pairs (Fig. 32) and train the
position probes of Sec. 5.2.1 on the diagonal settings (Fig. 33).
The results mirror the axis-aligned settings. Last-token patching shows the same three-phase pattern
with an ordering phase around layers 20–22; strip patching flips the prediction from the vision
embedding through layer ≈20; square-only patching has a weak effect, as it does for the axis-aligned
Squares setting on this model (Fig. 6); and, after ablating the vision-derived ordering information,
square-token patching flips the prediction in middle layers, with n reduced to 18–46 by the accuracy
of the ablated model. The diagonal probes reach 100% test accuracy, and their predictions again
spread along diagonal strips through the squares. The ordering mechanism is therefore not tied to the
horizontal and vertical axes of the token grid.

C.6.1

H OW A RE S PATIAL P OSITIONS R EPRESENTED ?
E XPERIMENTAL S ETTINGS

Building on the synthetic Squares setting described in Sec. 3.1, we construct two layouts, each
containing three equal-sized colored squares with fixed spacing. The two layouts differ only by a
shift: one layout is shifted left and the other is shifted right (or up and down), as illustrated in Fig. 34.
The layouts are designed such that the rightmost square in the left-shifted layout occupies the same
absolute image location as the leftmost square in the right-shifted layout. We refer to the square
occupying this shared location as the overlapping square.
We label the squares according to their ordinal position within the layout: left (0), middle (1), and
right (2). Under this labeling scheme, the overlapping square receives label 2 in the left-shifted layout
and label 0 in the right-shifted layout. Thus, the same absolute image location is associated with
different ordinal labels depending on the layout.
This construction allows us to distinguish ordinal ordering information from absolute-position
information. In particular, because the overlapping square appears at the same absolute image
location in both layouts but receives different ordinal labels, its label cannot be determined from
absolute position alone. The overlapping square therefore serves as the critical test case for whether
vision-encoder representations encode ordinal position within the layout, rather than only fixed image
coordinates.

left, middle, or right. In the extracted vision-encoder hidden states, each square corresponds to a
7 × 7 token region for Gemma and a 4 × 4 token region for Qwen.

Probing shows the presence of ordinal ordering information. We use 90 images for training and
30 held-out images for evaluation, generated from the square-color assignments described in Sec. 3.1.
Each color assignment is rendered in both layouts, and a single three-way linear probe is trained
jointly on visual-token representations from both layouts. This joint training ensures that the shared
absolute location receives conflicting ordinal labels across the two layouts. For instance, consider
the left and right-shifted configurations of the squares depicted in Fig. 34. Because the overlapping
location is assigned two different ordinal labels during training, any classifier that relies solely on
absolute image coordinates must fail. Successful prediction therefore requires information beyond
absolute position.
We evaluate this distinction by reporting, for the overlapping square, the probabilities assigned to its
two possible ordinal labels, P (0) and P (2), separately for each layout. The probabilities are averaged
over the visual tokens corresponding to the overlapping square and across all held-out images. For
completeness, we also report overall probe accuracies over all visual tokens corresponding to each of
the squares in Table 8.
As shown in Fig. 34, the probability assigned to the correct layout-dependent label increases across
vision-encoder layers in both models. These results show that ordinal ordering information is linearly
decodable from the vision-encoder representations of both models.

When do the strips emerge? The strip-like probe patterns of Fig. 4 are measured at the output of
the vision encoder and projector. To locate where they arise, we train a separate three-way position
probe on the object tokens at each vision-encoder layer (Squares setting; 90 training and 30 held-out
color permutations) and apply it to all tokens (Fig. 36, Table 10). The two models differ sharply in
timing. In Gemma-3-4b-it, whose SigLIP encoder adds learned absolute position embeddings to
the patch embeddings at the input, the layer-0 probe already reaches 100% held-out accuracy and
assigns probability 0.90 to the correct label on the background tokens of the object’s column, versus
0.20 elsewhere: the strips are present before any attention layer. In Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct,
whose encoder has no additive position embedding and injects position only through 2D rotary
embeddings inside attention, the layer-0 probe is at chance (29%) and shows no spatial structure;
ordering information and the strips emerge over the first five layers and saturate by layer 10. The
overlap-probe accuracies in Table 8 (Gemma 80.8% vs. Qwen 22.6% at layer 0) show the same
timing. This is consistent with the strips being a by-product of how position is made available
to attention: with absolute position embeddings, every token carries its own coordinates from the
input, and tokens that share a row or column coordinate share a component of their embedding; with
2D-RoPE, attention between tokens depends on their relative row and column offsets, so tokens
aligned with an object along one axis are the ones that attend to it most similarly, and position
information is propagated along rows and columns as attention layers accumulate. In both cases the
resulting representation is causally used by the LM (Sec. 5.2.2) and combines absolute and relative
components (Fig. 38).

Patching Vision Embeddings In addition to the probing experiments, we conducted interchange
intervention experiments to further characterize the ordinal information encoded by the vision encoder.
We perform these experiments as described in Fig. 37, where both the clean and counterfactual images
contain squares shifted in a particular direction, and the question asks for the color of the leftmost,
rightmost, topmost, or bottommost square. Specifically, if the clean image contains squares shifted to
the left, the counterfactual image shifts the squares to the right while reversing their order. We ensure
that the rightmost square in the left-shifted configuration is positioned at the center of the image, and
similarly for the other shifted configurations.
The intervention is performed over the entire stripe containing the square of the target color. We perform an interchange intervention by replacing the vision-encoder embeddings of the stripe containing
the queried square in the clean image with the corresponding stripe from the mirrored counterfactual.
This intervention yields different predictions depending on the representation encoded by the vision
encoder. If the vision embeddings encode ordinal information, then after patching, the model output
should change to the color of the square at the opposite end of the ordering. For example, after
applying the patching shown in Fig. 37, the output should change from blue to red if the vision
encoder encodes ordinal information. In contrast, if the vision encoder encodes absolute positional
information, then the output should change from blue to green, because the absolute coordinate values
of the intervened blue and red squares would be lower than those of the unintervened green square.
Fig. 38 shows the intervention experiment results. Surprisingly, the expected output for both absolute
and ordinal information is substantial, indicating that the vision encoder is forming both types of
positional information to encode spatial information. The exception is the vertical configuration with
the Gemma model, where patching the output of the vision encoder does not change the final output,
indicating a lack of ordering information, consistent with results in Fig. 21.
However, when the same experiment is performed on the LM-backbone ordering information using
the setup described in Section 5.3.2, as shown in Fig. 39, we primarily observe the expected output
corresponding to the relative ordering information. This suggests that the ordering information
encoded by the LM backbone is predominantly relative in nature, consistent with findings from prior
studies on LMs (Prakash et al., 2024; 2025; Assouel et al., 2025).

C OMPARISON TO CHAIN - OF - THOUGHT PROMPTING

A natural question is whether the failures corrected by ordering amplification could be recovered
simply by prompting. We evaluate the unmodified models on the same COCO-spatial items under a
chain-of-thought (CoT) prompt that asks the model to reason step by step and end with “Answer:
<word>” (greedy decoding), using the same strict two-ordering protocol. Unlike the amplification
sweep, which re-evaluates only the baseline failures and assumes baseline-correct items are kept,
CoT changes the prompt for every item, so we re-evaluate the full set; for comparison we also report
the amplification at its operating point (α=6) re-evaluated on the full set. Table 15 shows that the
effect of CoT is model-dependent: it helps Qwen2-VL-2B (0.600→0.752), correcting many failures
but also breaking three times as many previously correct items as the amplification does, and it is
net harmful for Gemma-3-4b (0.527→0.464), where it breaks 44% of previously correct items and
vertical accuracy collapses from 0.559 to 0.385. Inspecting Gemma’s generations, the spatial percepts
in the reasoning are often correct, but the final answer is bound to the wrong referent (e.g., “the giraffe

Amplifying the ordering directions perturbs every image token, so it could degrade capabilities
unrelated to spatial reasoning. We measure this on seven non-spatial VQA benchmarks (POPE, GQA,
MMBench, OK-VQA, VizWiz, AI2D, ScienceQA-IMG; 500 items each) by applying the both-axis
amplification at the paper’s operating point α=6 and comparing to α=0 (Table 16). For in-domain

COCO-spatial we report the fraction of baseline failures repaired with the axis-specific probe, as in

E XPERIMENTAL RESULTS WITH REALISTIC BACKGROUND

We also conduct probing experiments (Sec. 5.2.1) and intervention experiments (Sec. 5.2.2) using real
background images to demonstrate that ordering information remains distributed across background
tokens in real-world settings. We tested 15 different realistic backgrounds, and found a consistent
behavior across all.
Figures 66–77 present the probing results, indicating that ordering information is spread across
background tokens even when using real background images, rather than a white background.
Additionally, the results in figures 78-81 confirm that patching the strip is required to alter the
ordering information generated by the vision encoder.
