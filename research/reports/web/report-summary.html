Morning brief · summary of the full report

The model usually still knows. It just doesn't act on it.

When a model acts against what it knows, the knowledge mostly survives inside it. It loses out in the last layers, where the answer gets decided.

17 October 2026 · Based on 9 research notes, a reviewer audit, and a fact-check of about 150 claims · Full report

The picture in one diagram

Simplified picture of the best-supported case (sycophancy and similar gaps, mostly in models up to 14B). The knowledge isn't deleted; something else controls the output.

What happens to the knowledge: six possibilities, ranked by evidence

OutcompetedBest supported. The "this is wrong" signal is still there; a stronger "go along" signal wins.
BypassedModerate. The knowledge sits off to the side, in a direction the answer doesn't read. Mostly correlational.
SuppressedModerate on simple tasks, weak for sycophancy (inferred, not tested causally).
CancelledOne small-model paper. Probably under-detected, since most papers report only net effects.
Lost or transformedWeak. Real loss shows up only after deliberate unlearning, not under pressure.
ErasedThe one strong claim (2607.00415) looks like a probe artifact. See below.

No single paper tests all six on the same cases. That missing protocol is the opening for direction 1.

Why the "erasure" result is probably an illusion

Step 1Probe trained on normal answersWithout pressure, the correct answer and the model's next answer are the same letter. So the probe may just learn "what the model is about to say".
Step 2Tested under pressureOn items where the model caves, it is about to say the hinted (wrong) letter. The probe follows that.
ResultLooks like "the truth is gone"Accuracy drops below chance. Another paper (2609.10739) shows this exact pattern appears even when the truth is fully intact.

Checking this costs a few days on one GPU, and it's the top-ranked first experiment.

The evidence stops right where Dr. Lin wants to work

At 70B, the clearest "detect, then override" signature disappears under the usual tools. A careful 27–70B study is new ground, not a replication.

Verdicts on your three candidate directions

Partially done

1. Bypass vs override

Open but crowded at the edges. Closest competitors: 2609.04582, the KnownLieBench pilot and KAPPA (2509.23782: knowledge and prediction subspaces drift to near-orthogonal late, and aligning them fixes answers at 7–8B). Still new: one protocol that tells all the fates apart, including an output-null test, at 27–70B.

Open

2. Where shared geometry breaks

Low scoop risk, but a real chance of a null result. One small pilot found geometry didn't beat linear. Only makes sense on graded variables like authority level.

Partly scooped

3. Jump vs drift

A COLM 2026 paper claims "gradual drift", but from group averages and escalating pressure. A sharper per-dialogue test is still possible. Do this one later.

What's actionable

  • Fix the decision, not the belief. Simple linear steering already repairs yes/no "act or don't" gaps when it targets the decision.
  • Keep monitors out of training gradients. Training directly against a probe teaches the model to hide. Frozen probes used as rewards held up in the authors' own tests.
  • Train probes on mixed data. Probes fit only on normal answers can fake "the knowledge is gone".
  • Neurons for the action side. Override neurons look sparse and easy to switch off. Knowledge signals look spread out. Cammarata's essay is promising but unreplicated.
